\section{Results}

This chapter presents the empirical results. Section 4.1 describes the characteristics of the sample and the ownership sorted portfolios. Section 4.2 reports the main Fama French five factor regression results for the global equal weighted portfolios, which provide the primary test of Hypotheses H1 and H2. Section 4.3 presents the six factor robustness check for the third hypothesis. Section 4.4 reports the regional analysis for H4 and H5. Section 4.5 presents the robustness tests for static ownership measurement, alternative sorting, and value weighting. Section 4.6 summarises the hypothesis outcomes. Interpretation of the findings is discussed in the conclusion.


\subsection{Sample Characteristics}

Table 1 reports the sample characteristics. Panel A shows the distribution of the 1,285 sample firms across the five ownership quintiles and the four regions. The quintile boundaries lie at 26.5\%, 34.0\%, 43.7\%, and 56.8\% insider ownership. The mean ownership level rises from 23.2\% in P1 to 69.3\% in P5, with the highest ownership quintile spanning a wide range from 56.9\% to 99.8\%. Each quintile contains between 255 and 258 firms. The regional composition reflects the global dominance of concentrated ownership: Asia-Pacific accounts for 962 firms, North America for 141, Europe for 109, and emerging markets outside Asia for 73. This distribution is consistent across quintiles, with Asia-Pacific dominating each ownership level.

Panel B of Table 1 reports the summary statistics for the equal weighted portfolio returns over the 132-month sample period. Average monthly returns are positive in every quintile and rise with ownership concentration except for P4. Monthly returns from 1.53\% in P1 to 1.92\% in P5, with P3 reaching 1.87\% and P4 dipping back to 1.58\%. The annualized mean return is 18.31\% for P1 and 22.99\% for P5. The HML spread portfolio earns an average monthly return of 0.39\%, corresponding to 4.68\% per year. Monthly return standard deviations lie between 4.17\% and 4.98\% across the five quintile portfolios. The HML spread substantially less volatile at 2.30\% per month, reflecting the diversifying effect of the long short construction. Annualized sharpe ratios, computed as the ratio of mean return to standard deviation scaled by the square root of twelve. The sharpe rises from 1.12 in P1 to 1.59 in P5, with a Sharpe ratio of 0.59 on the HML spread. These raw return statistics provide an initial indication of the ownership-return relationship before any risk adjustment is applied.

\subsection{Fama French Five Factor Alpha by Ownership Quintile}

Table 2 reports the central result of the thesis. Five factor alphas are estimated for each of the five ownership sorted equal weighted portfolios and for the HML spread over the period January 2015 to December 2025. All five quintile portfolios earn positive and statistically significant five factor alphas at the 1\% level. The monthly alphas are 0.924\% for P1 (t = 3.224), 1.135\% for P2 (t = 3.323), 1.378\% for P3 (t = 4.239), 1.090\% for P4 (t = 3.026), and 1.454\% for P5 (t = 4.934). On an annualized basis, these alphas range from 11.09\% for P1 to 17.45\% for P5. The HML spread earns a monthly alpha of 0.530\%, statistically significant at the 1\% level with a Newey-West t-statistic of 2.734. This corresponds to an annualized risk adjusted spread of 6.36\% per year between the highest- and lowest ownership quintiles within the universe.

Two features of these results need attention. The alpha is positive and significant across all five quintile portfolios, including the lowest ownership quintile (P1). The universe of Wood (2025) therefore earns risk adjusted returns relative to the developed markets factor benchmark, not only the high ownership tail. Next to that, the progression from P1 to P5 is increasing but not exactly monotonic: P4 (1.090\%) falls below P3 (1.378\%). The breakdown in monotonicity is confined to a single ownership range and does not undermine the overall pattern, as confirmed by the significant HML spread and the cleaner monotonicity found in the decile sort below.

The factor loadings need further context. Market betas range from 0.530 to 0.754, indicating that the high ownership portfolios move less with the global market than the average developed market stock. SMB loadings are substantial between 0.603 and 0.828, reflecting the fact that high ownership firms skew toward smaller market capitalizations even within the one billion dollar minimum filter. CMA loadings are consistently negative, between -0.130 and -0.329, indicating that the portfolios are leaning toward firms that invest aggressively rather than conservatively. HML and RMW loadings are smaller in magnitude and less consistent in sign. Adjusted R-squared values range from 0.376 to 0.582, indicating that the five factors explain a moderate but not dominant share of return variation in the ownership sorted portfolios.


\subsection{Robustness to Momentum: Six Factor Results}

Table 3 reports the six factor regression results, in which the Carhart (1997) momentum factor is added to the five factor specification. If the documented ownership alpha reflected exposure to past return momentum, it would move toward zero once the momentum factor is included. The results show this is not the case. The six factor HML alpha is 0.528\% per month with a t-statistic of 2.792, identical to the five factor estimate of 0.530\%. The individual quintile alphas also remain positive and significant at the 1\% level, with monthly values of 0.926\%, 1.123\%, 1.395\%, 1.090\%, and 1.454\% from P1 to P5 respectively. Momentum loadings on the HML spread are small and statistically insignificant, indicating that the ownership sorted portfolios are not systematically exposed to momentum. The ownership alpha is therefore robust to the inclusion of momentum, providing support for hypothesis H3.

\subsection{Regional Analysis}

Table 4 reports the five factor regression results separately for the four regional subsamples. Each region is evaluated against its own regional Fama French factor set. Mentioning the exception of emerging markets outside Asia, for which the global developed markets factors are used as a proxy. The results provide a mixed picture of the cross regional robustness of the ownership premium.

In North America (Panel A, 141 firms), the HML alpha is 1.768\% per month. This corresponds to 21.22\% per year, statistically significant at the 10\% level with a Newey-West t-statistic of 1.681. The P5 portfolio in North America earns a monthly alpha of 2.420\%, the largest in any region. The signal is therefore strongest in North America, although the small sample size results in a t-statistic that does not clear the 5\% threshold.

In Europe (Panel B, 109 firms), the HML alpha is -0.133\% per month, statistically indistinguishable from zero (t = -0.468). The individual European quintile portfolios all earn positive and significant alphas between 0.540\% and 1.006\% per month. Showing that European high ownership firms as a group earn risk adjusted excess returns relative to the European factor benchmark. The absence of a significant HML spread, means that ownership concentration within the European subsample does not generate additional risk adjusted returns. The difference between the North American and European HML alphas is an interesting regional finding of the analysis.

In Asia-Pacific (Panel C, 962 firms), the HML alpha is 0.422\% per month with a t-statistic of 1.633. This is positive but not significant. All five quintile portfolios earn significant alphas between 1.361\% and 1.789\% per month, again indicating a strong Wood universe level effect but a weak ownership concentration effect. In emerging markets outside Asia (Panel D, 73 firms), the HML alpha is 0.320\% per month with a t-statistic of 0.944, also not significant. The small sample size in this region limits the power of the test, and the use of global factors as a proxy gives additional uncertainty.

Taken together, the regional analysis provides partial support for hypothesis H4 and supports hypothesis H5. Positive alpha is documented in every region for every quintile portfolio. The HML spread, however, is statistically significant only in North America, where high insider ownership is most rare. The fifth hypothesis, that the North American ownership alpha exceeds the European ownership alpha, is confirmed by a solid margin: 1.768\% versus -0.133\% per month at the HML level.


\subsection{Robustness Tests}


\subsubsection{Split Sample Stability}

Table 6 reports the five factor results for the two periods, January 2015 to December 2019 (60 months) and January 2020 to December 2025 (72 months). The HML spread is positive and statistically significant in both halves. In the pre-COVID period the HML alpha is 0.764\% per month (t = 2.393, significant at the 5\% level), corresponding to 9.16\% per year. In the post-COVID period the HML alpha is 0.434\% per month (t = 2.272, also significant at the 5\% level), corresponding to 5.21\% per year. The pre-COVID alpha is larger than the post-COVID alpha. This is the opposite of what would be expected if the 2025 ownership snapshot were artificially driving results through look ahead bias. The stability of the HML alpha across the two sub periods supports the use of static ownership sorting. It provides further evidence that the documented premium is not an effect of end of sample measurement.

\subsubsection{Alternative Portfolio Sorts}

Moving on to the results of the two alternative sort specifications. Panel A of table 7 reports the tercile sort, in which the universe is divided into three equal size groups. The tercile HML alpha is 0.382\% per month, significant at the 5\% level (t = 2.174). The three tercile alphas are 0.993\% (T1), 1.257\% (T2), and 1.374\% (T3), a monotonic pattern from low to high ownership. The different tercile sort therefore produces a cleaner monotonic pattern than the quintile sort, where P4 dipped below P3.

Panel B of Table 7 reports the decile sort, in which the universe is divided into ten approximately equal size portfolios of around 128 firms each. The decile sort provides a detailed view of the ownership-return relationship across the full ownership distribution. Ownership ranges at the low end (D1: 20.0\% to 23.4\%, D2: 23.4\% to 26.4\%) and at the top end (D10: 68.4\% to 99.8\%). All ten decile portfolios earn positive and statistically significant five factor alphas at the 1\% level, with monthly values ranging from 0.99\% in D1 to 1.63\% in D10. The HML spread between the highest and lowest deciles is 0.630\% per month, significant at the 5\% level (t = 2.190), corresponding to an annualized spread of 7.57\%. Figure 1 in the appendix illustrates the decile alpha pattern visually, showing the upward trend across the ten deciles with the linear trend line showing the monotonic pattern. The decile HML spread is approximately 19\% larger than the quintile HML spread of 0.530\%, reflecting the wider ownership differential between D1 (20\% to 23\%) and D10 (68\% to 100\%) compared with P1 (20\% to 27\%) and P5 (57\% to 100\%).

\subsubsection{Value Weighted Portfolios}

Table 8 presents the five factor regression results using value weighted portfolio returns. The value weighted HML spread alpha is 1.977\% per month (t = 2.204), significant at the 5\% level and corresponding to 23.72\% per year. This estimate is substantially larger than the equal weighted HML alpha of 0.530\% per month. The value weighted P5 portfolio earns a monthly alpha of 2.571\%, significant at the 1\% level (t = 3.091). The other value weighted quintile alphas are smaller in and less significant than their equal weighted counterparts: 0.594\% in P1 (significant at the 10\% level), 0.362\% in P2 (insignificant), 1.441\% in P3 (significant at the 10\% level), and 0.201\% in P4 (insignificant). The value weighted regressions also display lower adjusted R-squared values, indicating that the global factors explain a smaller share of return variation when weights are concentrated in a small number of large firms. The reduced significance of individual quintile alphas under value weighting reflects the higher portfolio return volatility that results from this concentration rather than a weaker underlying premium. The value weighted results therefore confirm that the ownership premium is not a result of equal weighting smaller firms within each portfolio.
