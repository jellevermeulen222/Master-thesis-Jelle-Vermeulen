\section{Introduction}

The separation of ownership and control is a discussed features of public markets and one of the oldest concerns in corporate finance. When managers own little of the firm they run, their incentives may diverge from those of outside shareholders. The agency costs theory is first formalized by Jensen and Meckling (1976). A simple implication of agency theory is that concentrated insider ownership is that aligning the interests of those who control the firm with those who own it. This should be associated with better firm performance. The question is, if this alignment also translates into superior stock returns for investors.

A substantial part of the literature has examined the relationship between insider ownership and firm value. The specific evidence on stock returns is narrower and largely focussed on specific subsets of insiders and single country samples. Fahlenbrach (2009) documents that founder chief executive firms in the United States earn positive risk-adjusted returns. Lilienfeld-Toal and Ruenzi (2014) find a comparable premium for chief executive ownership, also in the United States. These papers are important building blocks of the research done in this paper. The most important extension is made on the Wood (2025) paper, which shows that owner operated firms worldwide have compounded at substantially higher rates than the broad market over three decades. Wood's evidence is descriptive and based on raw returns. It does not prove whether the documented outperformance survives adjustment for the systematic risk factors that are known to explain cross sectional return differences. This leaves open the central question of whether the outperformance of high ownership firms reflects a genuine risk-adjusted premium or that this excess return is compensation for exposure to size, value, profitability, or investment risks.

This thesis addresses that part by asking if aggregate insider ownership predicts risk-adjusted stock returns in a globally diversified sample. Using 1,285 firms drawn from the Wood (2025) universe, defined by insider ownership exceeding 20\% and a market capitalization above one billion US dollars. The analysis used in this paper sorts firms into ownership ranked portfolios and estimates Fama French five factor alphas over the period January 2015 to December 2025. Unlike the previous literature, the sorting variable is aggregate insider ownership rather than the equity held by any single individual. Alongside this, the sample contains Asia-Pacific, North America, Europe, and emerging markets rather than a single country. The robustness of the results is assessed through a six factor specification, a split sample analysis, alternative tercile and decile sorts, and value weighted portfolios. To separate the high presence of Asia-Pacific and compare the market environment behaviours, the relationship is examined separately within each region against the corresponding regional factors.

The results show that every ownership sorted portfolio earns a positive and statistically significant five factor alpha. This indicates that the high ownership universe as a whole outperforms the global benchmark on a risk-adjusted basis. Within that universe, the premium increases with ownership concentration. Measured as the spread between the highest and lowest ownership portfolios, which  is positive, statistically significant, and robust to all four sets of robustness tests. The premium is strongest in North America, where high insider ownership is rare, and statistically insignificant in Europe, where concentrated ownership is common. These findings extend the portfolio sort methodology of Fahlenbrach (2009) and Lilienfeld-Toal and Ruenzi (2014) to a global setting and to the aggregate insider ownership universe. Confirming Wood's (2025) descriptive findings in the risk-adjusted setting, and connect the return based ownership literature to the cross-country institutional evidence of La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, and Shleifer (1999).

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews the theoretical and empirical literature on insider ownership, firm value, and stock returns. This chapter also develops the five hypotheses tested in the analysis. Chapter 3 describes the data and the portfolio sort methodology. Chapter 4 presents the empirical results. Chapter 5 discusses the interpretation of the findings, their relation to the competing theoretical perspectives, and the limitations of the study. Chapter 6 concludes.
